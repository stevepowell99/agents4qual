Rubicon report

In a random sample of 12 interviews (3 per borough, seeded draw), how far do young people's first-hand accounts of loneliness support or contradict four theories: discrepancy (quality not quantity of ties), Weiss's emotional versus social loneliness, Cacioppo's self-reinforcing withdrawal loop, and social media as displacing versus compensating for face-to-face contact?

Of the four theories, discrepancy theory fits these accounts best: interviews support it outright, show it alongside plain absence of people, and contradicts it. The other three theories find support in a minority of interviews and are silent in many, so the sample can say little for or against them. The sample is interviews drawn at random, three from each borough, and every count below rests only on what respondents said about their own lives.

Discrepancy: quality of ties, not their number

Most respondents describe being lonely with people around them. One felt far from family he lived with: "Even though I see them, probably like, 23 hours in a day, I just feel like I was so far away from everyone" . Another had company for nights out but not for hard times: "I was around people but just not around the right people" . Others speak of "temporary friends, as opposed to real friends" and of not being able to talk to friends "because you feel like they might not understand you" .

The one contradicting account puts loneliness down to an empty room: "Just someone sitting on another bed or… you won’t feel lonely" . The two mixed accounts add a lonely spell after a house move, "because I didn't have anyone there" , and night-time thoughts that come "purely because no one is around" . So plain absence of people also matters, but in this sample it is the lesser story.

Weiss: emotional against social loneliness

interviews support Weiss and show both support and contradiction; contradict him outright, and give no evidence either way. In support, one respondent felt close to his family but lonely at college, where "you’re surrounded by people but you don’t know any of them really" . The mixed cases are telling: one man missed "just one person you can talk to" while he had friends and family, then describes his girlfriend filling the gap when friends were busy ; one woman lacked a confidant among her "party people" yet also says "You can have a family and don’t have friends, you’re still fine" . That second half cuts against Weiss's claim that one kind of tie cannot stand in for the other.

Cacioppo: the self-reinforcing loop

interviews support the loop, show both withdrawal and reaching out, contradicts it, and give no first-hand account of what the respondent did when lonely. The clearest support: "I physically trapped myself in my room because I didn’t wanna communicate with people", which "kinda prolonged the experience" ; the same respondent later recovered "through my best friend and football and then my mates" . Another left a park when others came in, and says "you remove yourself then you feel lonely" . The contradicting respondent says loneliness "wouldn’t last" because she would "make up with this person or speak my mind" . Several respondents describe the loop clearly but only as what "you" would do, which this coding does not count . Read as a whole, the accounts fit Cacioppo's fuller model, in which loneliness pushes both ways, better than the loop alone.

Social media: displacement against compensation

The two hypotheses come out close: interviews describe displacement, compensation, both, and tie social media to nothing in their own loneliness. Displacement here is mostly comparison and exclusion rather than lost time: "when you see pictures that you're not involved in and it just generally seems fun, that's when you start feeling alone" , and phones at the table that "kind of takes that connection away" . Compensation is about reaching people who are far away or not there: relatives abroad , or the phone at night as "the only way I can stop that from happening" . The one respondent coded both names lying in bed on Instagram as his loneliest place but credits his phone with why he does not feel lonely elsewhere .

What this sample cannot show

  • The interview task asked what respondents associate with loneliness, which invites talk of misunderstanding and quality; the strong showing for discrepancy theory may partly reflect the questions asked.
  • A small random sample cannot give prevalence, and the theories with many no-evidence cases (Weiss, the loop) are untested here rather than refuted: the interviews were not designed to probe them.
  • One coder read each interview, with no second coder; the strict first-hand rule set several vivid general statements aside, and a broader rule would raise the support counts for the loop in particular.

How this was made

The instruction a second coder would follow
You are reading one interview with a young person (18 to 24) in East London about loneliness. Part 1 asks what they associate with loneliness; part 2 asks about places where they feel most connected and most lonely. Decide how the interview bears on one theory. Discrepancy theory (Perlman and Peplau): loneliness is a gap between the relationships a person wants and the ones they have, especially in their quality (being understood, depth, closeness), not simply their number. Count only the respondent's own experience of loneliness or connection (first-hand: what happened to them, what they felt, what they did). A general view about young people or 'people' with no tie to the respondent's own life does not count and goes to no_evidence. Read the whole interview, both parts, before deciding. Quote the single passage that best carries the value, copied exactly; for no_evidence quote the passage that comes closest to the theory, or the respondent's first answer if nothing comes close.
